How to Feel at Home in a New Country

By Stephanie Johnson, LICSW, a licensed clinical social worker and expat

Everyone tells you how to set up a new life abroad. Find the apartment, open the bank account, learn the transit map. Almost no one tells you how to feel at home once all the boxes are checked, because that part cannot be arranged in an afternoon. It arrives on its own schedule, quietly, and often long after you assumed it should have.

If you are somewhere new and it still does not feel like yours, you are not behind and you are not doing it wrong. Feeling at home is not a task you complete. It is something that grows through ordinary days, and the growing takes longer than anyone warns you. Let me tell you what actually helps it along.

Home is built, not found

The first thing worth letting go of is the idea that you will one day arrive somewhere and simply feel at home, the way you might find the right coat. That is not how it works. Belonging is not something you find fully formed. It is something you build slowly, one small familiar thing at a time, until a foreign place has quietly accumulated enough of you to feel like yours.

Belonging is quieter than you expect. One day the walk home just feels like yours, and you cannot say exactly when it started.

That means the early emptiness is not a sign the place is wrong for you. It is simply the sign that you have not lived enough ordinary days there yet. Home is made of repetition, and repetition takes time.

Why it takes longer than you expect

There is a real reason a new country stays tiring and unfamiliar for a while. Your body reads constant newness as mild alertness, and it stays a little braced until enough of your surroundings become predictable. Every unfamiliar street, language, and social rule asks for attention that used to be automatic. Feeling at home is partly the moment your body finally stops bracing, and that moment cannot be rushed. It can only be built toward.

So be patient with the timeline. Most people feel a real shift somewhere in the first several months to a year, not in the first few weeks, and the dip in the middle is normal rather than a warning sign.

The small things that turn a place into yours

You do not build belonging in milestones. You build it in routines. A few things reliably help.

  1. Find your third place. Not home, not work. One cafe, gym, park, or shop you return to often enough that the faces start to know yours. Belonging is built by repetition, and a single familiar spot is often where it begins.
  2. Build small daily rituals. The same morning walk, the same market on Saturdays, a coffee from the same person. Predictable little rituals are not boring. They are how you signal to your own body that you are safe here, and safety is the ground that home grows in.
  3. Learn the ordinary rhythms, not just the sights. Ask locals what they do on a normal Sunday, where they walk, what they eat, how they rest. The everyday texture of a place makes you feel at home far faster than any landmark.
  4. Let yourself be a regular somewhere. The day the barista starts your order before you ask is a small ceremony of belonging. Give a few places the chance to recognize you.
  5. Make one or two real connections. A single person who knows a little of your story changes how a whole city feels. Home is often less a place than the people in it who know your name.
  6. Bring a few pieces of the old home with you. A recipe, a song, a small object, a familiar habit. You do not have to abandon where you came from to belong where you are. The two can live side by side.

The settling click

There is a moment most people abroad eventually notice, though rarely on the day it happens. An unremarkable Tuesday when the grocery store stops feeling like an escape room, the walk home feels automatic, and the city begins to recognize you before you realize you have started calling it home. That is the settling click, the quiet day a foreign place becomes yours. You cannot force it and you cannot schedule it. You can only keep building the ordinary days that make room for it. Feeling at home is not a switch you flip. It is a slow accumulation, and one day you look up and realize it has already happened. Be gentle with yourself while it grows. You are not failing to arrive. You are simply still arriving, which is exactly how it is supposed to feel.

Common questions

How long does it take to feel at home in a new country?

For most people it is months rather than weeks, and often closer to a year before a place truly feels like theirs. There is usually a harder dip in the middle before it eases. The timeline varies, but feeling unsettled for a while is completely normal, not a sign you chose wrong.

Why do I still not feel at home after moving abroad?

Because home is built through repetition, and you may simply not have lived enough ordinary days there yet. Your body stays a little braced amid constant newness until enough becomes familiar. Small daily routines and a few regular places are what slowly turn the unfamiliar into home.

What actually helps you feel at home somewhere new?

Routines and repetition more than milestones. Find a third place you return to often, build small daily rituals, learn the ordinary rhythms of the place, let yourself become a regular, and make one or two real connections. Belonging accumulates quietly out of these small, repeated things.
