The inner side of moving abroad

The Expat Identity Crisis: Who Am I Now That I've Moved Abroad?

By Stephanie Johnson, LICSW, a licensed clinical social worker and expat

There is a strange, disorienting stretch that catches a lot of people some months into life abroad. You look up from the daily logistics and realize you are not quite sure who you are anymore. The version of you that everyone knew, the one with the reputation and the role and the easy shorthand, is on the other side of the world, and the person standing here in a new country has not fully taken shape yet.

If that hollow, in-between feeling has found you, you are not losing your mind and you are not losing yourself. You are having one of the most common and least talked about experiences of living abroad. Let me explain why it happens, and why it is not the emergency it can feel like.

Why moving abroad shakes your sense of self

We tend to think of identity as something we carry entirely on the inside. In truth, a great deal of who we feel ourselves to be is held up by the world around us. Your job title, your role in your family, the friends who reflect you back to yourself, the language you are witty in, the streets you know by heart, the culture whose rules you can read without thinking. All of that is scaffolding, and it quietly tells you who you are all day long.

When you move abroad, that scaffolding comes down almost overnight. Nobody here knows your history. Your competence does not transfer cleanly. You cannot be funny in the same way, or read the room the same way, or move through the world on autopilot. It is not that you changed. It is that everything that used to reflect you back disappeared at once.

Sometimes you do not miss a place. You miss the version of yourself that existed there.

Feeling out of place is not the same as being lost

Here is the reframe that matters most. The disorientation you feel is not evidence that you have lost yourself. It is the completely predictable result of losing the external structure that used to make your identity feel obvious and effortless. You are not broken. You are between versions, standing in the gap after the old scaffolding came down and before the new life has grown up around you.

That gap is uncomfortable, and it is also, quietly, an opening. When the world stops telling you who you are, you get a rare chance to ask yourself the question directly, without all the old expectations answering it for you.

The one thing that came with you

This is the heart of it, and it is the thing I most want you to hold. Being grounded has very little to do with where you are. It is about who you believe you are and how you want to show up in the world. A place, a role, a familiar crowd can make that easy to feel, but they were never actually the source of it.

Your values came with you. What you love, what you want, the way you mean to treat people, the things that matter to you at three in the morning, none of that stayed behind at the airport. The scaffolding fell away, but the structure underneath it is still standing. In a country that does not know you yet, you are still the one familiar thing you have. Steady was never coming from the place. It was coming from you, and it made the trip.

Rebuilding who you are, on your own terms

Identity abroad does not come back all at once. It reassembles slowly, the same way home does, through the small choices you make about how to live now. A few things help it along.

  • Anchor to your values, not your old roles. When you cannot lean on your title or your reputation, ask what you actually care about and let that lead. Values are portable. Roles are not.
  • Notice who you are becoming, not only what you lost. Living abroad quietly grows new parts of you: more resourceful, more open, braver in ways your old life never asked for. Some of the best of you only exists because you left.
  • Let yourself be a beginner. You are learning to be yourself in a new context, at whatever age you are. That is humbling, and it is also its own kind of courage.
  • Give the questions somewhere to go. Write them down instead of circling them at night. Reflection turns a vague identity crisis into something you can actually sit with and move through.

One day, and you will not notice the exact moment, you will realize you have stopped translating everything, including yourself. You will feel like you again, not the old you exactly, but a fuller one, made of both where you came from and where you chose to go. You did not lose yourself abroad. You are just becoming someone your old life did not have room for. Be patient with that person. They are worth the wait.

Common questions

Is it normal to feel like you lost yourself after moving abroad?

Yes, and it is far more common than people admit. So much of our identity is held up by external things: roles, relationships, language, familiar surroundings. When those fall away in a move, the disorientation that follows is a normal response, not a sign that something is wrong with you.

Why do I feel like a different person since living abroad?

Partly because the world that used to reflect your identity back to you is gone, and partly because living abroad genuinely grows new parts of you. You are both missing the old scaffolding and becoming someone fuller. Both of those can feel disorienting at once.

How do you rebuild your identity after moving abroad?

By anchoring to your values rather than your old roles, noticing who you are becoming instead of only what you left, allowing yourself to be a beginner, and giving the hard questions a place to land through reflection. Identity abroad reassembles slowly, through the daily choices about how you want to live now.

This article is educational and reflective, not therapy, diagnosis, or a crisis service. If you are struggling beyond what a journal can hold, please reach out to someone you trust, a licensed professional, or local emergency services. You deserve real support.
