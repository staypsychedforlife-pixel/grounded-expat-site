How to Say Goodbye Before You Move Abroad

By Stephanie Johnson, LICSW, a licensed clinical social worker and expat

In the weeks before a move abroad, almost all the attention goes to logistics. The visa, the shipping, the lease, the endless list. Underneath it, quietly, something much harder is happening. You are saying goodbye. To people, to a home, to a whole chapter of your life, and often to a version of yourself that only existed there. Almost no one prepares you for how much that costs.

If the goodbyes ahead of you feel heavier than you expected, you are not being dramatic and you are not having second thoughts. You are grieving, in advance, something real. Let me help you do it consciously, so the leaving does not happen in a blur you later wish you could go back and feel.

The grief that comes before the plane

We usually think of grief as something that follows a loss. But when you know a goodbye is coming, the grief often arrives early. It is called anticipatory grief, and it is why you might find yourself tearful at an ordinary dinner, or aching at your own going-away party, or oddly short with the people you love most in the final weeks. Your heart has started letting go before your body has left.

Every new beginning quietly asks you to grieve what you are leaving behind. Both can be true at once.

This does not mean you are making a mistake. Excitement and grief are not opposites, and they are not in competition. You can be genuinely thrilled to go and genuinely heartbroken to leave, in the same week, sometimes in the same hour. That is not confusion. That is the honest emotional shape of a big, chosen change.

Say goodbye consciously, not in a blur

The most common regret I hear from people after a move is that the leaving happened in a rush of packing and paperwork, and they never really got to feel it or mark it. You can protect yourself from that. Goodbyes go better when you let them be real instead of letting them get lost in logistics.

  1. Name what you are actually losing. Not just the people, but the ordinary things: the Sunday walk, the friend down the street, the cafe, the version of your days you will not have anymore. Naming a loss gives it somewhere to live besides the back of your throat.
  2. Have the real goodbyes, not just the busy ones. Make time for the conversations that matter, the ones where you actually say what someone has meant to you. You will not regret being too sincere. You may regret staying too busy to be.
  3. Mark it with a small ritual. A last walk through the neighborhood, a favorite meal, a letter you leave behind, a photo in the doorway. Rituals help the heart catch up to what the calendar already knows.
  4. Say goodbye to the places, too. Go back to the spots that shaped this chapter and let yourself feel them. Places hold our memories, and a conscious goodbye to a place is a real and worthy thing.
  5. Let the goodbye be imperfect. Not every relationship gets a clean ending, and not every goodbye will feel complete. Do what you can, and let the rest be enough. A messy goodbye is still a goodbye.

You carry them with you

Here is the truest thing I know about leaving. You do not actually leave the people and places you love at the airport. The goodbye is real, and so is the distance, and I am not going to tell you either one disappears. But the love goes with you. It travels in the recipes and the habits and the way you still carry a piece of every place that shaped you. Saying goodbye well is not about closing a door on where you came from. It is about honoring it fully enough that you can carry it forward, rather than dragging it behind you unspoken. You are allowed to grieve the chapter that is ending and still turn, when it is time, toward the one beginning. Be gentle with yourself in these last weeks. The heaviness is only the measure of how much there was to love.

Common questions

Why is saying goodbye before moving abroad so hard?

Because you are grieving a real loss in advance. Anticipatory grief is the sorrow that arrives before a goodbye you know is coming, and it can make the final weeks unexpectedly emotional. You are not having second thoughts, you are feeling the true weight of what you are leaving, which is a sign of how much it mattered.

How do you say goodbye to friends and family before moving abroad?

Consciously and sincerely, rather than in a blur of logistics. Make time for the real conversations, tell people what they have meant to you, and mark the leaving with a small ritual. Then set up a plan to stay connected across the distance, so the goodbye is a change in the relationship rather than an ending.

Is it normal to feel grief and excitement about moving abroad at the same time?

Completely normal. Excitement and grief are not opposites and can coexist fully. You can be thrilled to go and heartbroken to leave in the very same moment. Holding both is not a sign of doubt, it is the honest experience of a meaningful, chosen change.

This article is educational and reflective, not therapy, diagnosis, or a crisis service. If you are struggling beyond what a journal can hold, please reach out to someone you trust, a licensed professional, or local emergency services. You deserve real support.
